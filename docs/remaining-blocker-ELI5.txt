THE REMAINING BLOCKER - EXPLAINED IN PLAIN ENGLISH
================================================================================
Date written  : 2026-09-22
Branch        : upgrade/pic18f47q10
Chip under test : PIC18F47Q10  (replacing the older PIC16F18875)

Short answer : The amplifier trips itself off as soon as it is asked to transmit.
               It is not a wiring fault, not a PTT fault, and not the chip being
               broken. The protection logic decides something is wrong and folds
               the amplifier into a safe shutdown. We do not yet know WHICH
               protection fired, or why it fires on the new chip but not the old.

Written to be read cold by someone who was not in the session.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
READ THIS FIRST, IF YOU ONLY READ ONE THING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

There is no danger, and the chip is not faulty.

The controller's whole job is to protect an expensive linear amplifier. One of the
ways it protects it is by refusing to transmit - and then refusing to let you
transmit again until you clear the fault.

On the new chip, it is making that decision when it should not. So the amplifier
never keys. That is the blocker.


================================================================================
PART 1 - WHAT THE AMPLIFIER CONTROLLER DOES, AND WHY IT REFUSES TO TRANSMIT
================================================================================

The controller sits between a transceiver (the "radio") and a big linear amplifier
that boosts the signal to 1-2 kW. Its most important job is not to transmit - it is
to STOP transmitting when something is wrong, because the amplifier's output
transistors cost real money and fail instantly if driven badly.

So the firmware continuously checks a list of things and will "trip" (shut down and
latch a fault) if any of them looks wrong. The things it watches:

  SWR1 / SWR2    Standing Wave Ratio. A measure of how badly the antenna is matched.
                 High SWR means RF power is coming back down the feeder instead of
                 going out of the antenna, and that reflected power heats and can
                 destroy the amplifier. Two separate directional couplers are used
                 (SWR1 uses one pair of sensors, SWR2 the other).

  OVERDRIVE      Too much drive power going in from the radio.
  DRAIN          The peak voltage on the drain of the output transistor - too high
                 means it is about to be destroyed.
  CURRENT        Too much current being drawn - usually the sign of a short or a
                 failing transistor.
  TEMPERATURE    The heatsink is too hot.
  HWFAULT        The "hardware fault" line. On this board that is an EXTERNAL
                 comparator chip that watches for overcurrent and latches a signal
                 if it sees it. The firmware just reads that line.

When any of those trips, the firmware sets:

  g_fault_latched = true     (a flag meaning "a fault has been seen and is remembered")
  g_state         = STATE_TRIP (3), or STATE_FAULT_LATCHED (4) if it needs clearing

...and while that is set, the amplifier will not key. Deliberately. That is the
correct behaviour - if it is tripped for a real reason, refusing to transmit is
exactly what you want.


================================================================================
PART 2 - THE HARDWARE AND THE MEASUREMENTS FEEDING THOSE CHECKS
================================================================================

Most of the trip inputs are ANALOGUE - they are measured as a voltage by the chip's
ADC (Analog to Digital Converter) and then compared against thresholds in software.

THE ANALOGUE INPUTS
-------------------
Pin      Channel   Signal name            What it measures
-------  --------  ---------------------  ------------------------------------
RA0      AN0       ADC_SWR1_FWD           Forward RF power, coupler 1
RA1      AN1       ADC_SWR1_REF           Reflected RF power, coupler 1
RA2      AN2       ADC_SWR2_FWD           Forward RF power, coupler 2
RA3      AN3       ADC_SWR2_REF           Reflected RF power, coupler 2
RA5      AN4       ADC_TEMP               Heatsink temperature (thermistor)
RB1      AN10      ADC_CURRENT            Amplifier current draw
RB2      AN11      ADC_OVERDRIVE          Drive power level
RB3      AN12      ADC_DRAIN_PEAK         Peak drain voltage

The ADC on this chip only has ONE converter, shared between all these inputs. The
firmware therefore SCANS them: it measures one channel every 1 ms tick, cycling
through all eight. So a full set of readings takes 8 ms, and any single reading can
be up to 8 ms old when a trip decision is made.

THE DIGITAL INPUT
-----------------
Pin      Signal name              Notes
-------  -----------------------  --------------------------------------------
RB4      INPUT_OVERCURRENT_FAULT  From the EXTERNAL hardware comparator. Read as a
                                  plain 0 or 1, not measured as a voltage.

HOW A TRIP DECISION IS MADE
---------------------------
Each tick the firmware takes the latest ADC readings, converts them into real
units (volts, amps, watts, degrees C) and compares against two sets of thresholds
stored in its settings:

  * a WARNING threshold - shown on the display, no trip
  * a TRIP threshold    - shuts the amplifier down and latches the fault

The thresholds are per-band and stored in EEPROM, loaded at power-up.


================================================================================
PART 3 - WHAT THE NEW CORRECTED PROBE FOUND
================================================================================

An earlier test claimed the firmware was stuck in its startup wait forever. That was
wrong - a mistake in my own test arithmetic, not a firmware fault. (See the separate
ELI5 document, docs/pin-write-question-ELI5.txt, for that story.)

Once the test was corrected to run long enough, the picture changed completely. The
firmware now does all of this correctly:

    g_startup_inhibit = false    it finished its startup wait
    g_ptt_active      = true     it latched the PTT button press
    g_snoop_active    = true     it entered "bypass snoop" and started looking for RF

...and then, immediately afterwards:

    g_fault_latched   = true     IT TRIPPED
    g_state           = 3        STATE_TRIP

So the sequence is:

    power up  ->  startup wait  ->  PTT pressed  ->  looking for the band
              ->  TRIP!

The amplifier is being shut down by the protection logic at the moment it is asked
to transmit. That is why the test reported "0 keyed runs" - the amplifier is
deliberately refusing to key, because it believes it is faulty.


================================================================================
PART 4 - WHY THIS IS A REAL FINDING AND NOT ANOTHER TEST ARTEFACT
================================================================================

After the earlier mistakes, a fair question is "how do we know THIS one is real?"

Three reasons:

  1. A POSITIVE CONTROL proved the test can drive the inputs.
     The test fakes the PTT button press by typing a command at the simulator. That
     command was separately verified to work: pulling the PTT pin low changed what
     the chip sees, and releasing it changed it back (229 -> 228 -> 229 on the port).
     So the firmware really did receive the button press.

  2. The state values are read from the firmware's OWN variables.
     g_fault_latched and g_state are the firmware's internal bookkeeping. The test is
     not interpreting a voltage or guessing - it is reading the firmware's own
     conclusion, the same variable the firmware itself tests before keying.

  3. It is repeatable and stable, not a one-off.
     Every step of the probe shows the same trip state, and the trip persists.

So: the firmware is genuinely making a trip decision on this chip.


================================================================================
PART 5 - WHY THE SAME FIRMWARE DOES NOT DO THIS ON THE OLD CHIP
================================================================================

This is the interesting part, and the reason the "trip" needs investigating rather
than just resetting.

The old chip is a PIC16F18875. The new chip is a PIC18F47Q10. The firmware SOURCE is
the same for both. So a behaviour difference must come from a difference in how the
two chips behave, or in how the firmware was configured for each. The candidates:

  1. DIFFERENT ADC BEHAVIOUR.
     The two chips have different ADC hardware. Different reference settings,
     different acquisition timing, different channel numbering, different accuracy.
     If the Q10's ADC returns a different raw number for the same voltage, then a
     reading that sits comfortably below a trip threshold on the old chip could sit
     above it on the new one.

     This is the leading suspect. The simulator also emits an ADC warning repeatedly
     during runs ("W0223-ADC: ADC input voltage low. ADC output underflow"), which
     suggests the ADC is being read in a state the model considers unusual.

  2. UNINITIALISED OR DIFFERENTLY-INITIALISED ANALOGUE PINS.
     If an analogue pin is left in a state that reads as a large value, that reads as
     a huge "current" or "drain voltage" and trips immediately. The analogue-select
     registers differ between the two chip families, and one of the whole-register
     writes in the port setup may not mean the same thing on both.

  3. THRESHOLDS LOADED FROM EEPROM ARE NOT WHAT THE TESTS EXPECT.
     Trip thresholds live in EEPROM. The two chips have different EEPROM sizes and
     the NVM (non-volatile memory) driver was rewritten for the Q10. If the settings
     fail to load cleanly, the firmware may fall back to defaults - and a default of
     zero for a threshold means "trip on anything".

  4. THE EXTERNAL HARDWARE FAULT LINE.
     RB4 is read from an external comparator chip. On the bench with no amplifier
     connected, that line floats - and a floating input is not the same as a
     deliberate 0. If the simulator models it as asserted, the firmware sees a
     hardware overcurrent fault and trips correctly, but for a reason that does not
     exist on the real board.

  5. SIMULATOR FIDELITY.
     Least likely, but possible: the Q10 model's ADC may not behave like silicon.
     This would be the conclusion of last resort, and only after 1-4 are eliminated -
     this project has already had TWO wrong "the simulator can't do it" verdicts
     that both turned out to be firmware configuration nobody had written.


================================================================================
PART 6 - HOW WE WILL FIND OUT WHICH ONE IT IS
================================================================================

The firmware has a variable called g_trip_reason - a set of bits saying WHICH
protection fired. That is the single most valuable thing to read, and it turns a
vague "something tripped" into a precise answer.

The plan, in order:

  1. READ g_trip_reason. It is a bit field:
         bit 0 (0x01) SWR1
         bit 1 (0x02) SWR2
         bit 2 (0x04) HWFAULT
         bit 3 (0x08) CURRENT
         bit 4 (0x10) TEMPERATURE
         bit 5 (0x20) OVERDRIVE
         bit 6 (0x40) DRAIN
     One number immediately names the culprit.

  2. READ THE RAW ADC VALUES for each channel at the moment of the trip, alongside
     the threshold it is being compared against. That shows whether the fault is a
     genuinely different measurement or a threshold that loaded wrong.

  3. COMPARE AGAINST THE OLD CHIP. Run the identical probe on the 16F build and read
     the same variables. If the 16F ADC values differ from the Q10's for identical
     stimulus, that is the ADC or the pin setup - a firmware fix. If they agree but
     the trip differs, it is the threshold or the decision logic.

This is deliberately the same method that the earlier mistakes taught us to use:
measure the input, measure the decision, and compare against a known-good case,
rather than reasoning about what "should" happen.


================================================================================
PART 7 - WHAT THIS DOES AND DOES NOT MEAN
================================================================================

DOES NOT MEAN:
  * The chip is broken.
  * The port has failed.
  * There is any danger to the hardware - quite the opposite, the protection is
    working, it is just working too eagerly.
  * The PTT path is faulty - PTT, startup and snoop all proved correct.

DOES MEAN:
  * The Q10 port is very close: everything up to the trip works.
  * One trip input is reading differently on the new chip than the old one.
  * The next step is a measurement, not a guess: read g_trip_reason.
  * Until this is resolved, the suite cannot pass, because the suite requires the
    amplifier to key and the amplifier is refusing to key.

SAFETY STATUS - the reassuring part
-----------------------------------
The full test suite checks six safety invariants, and ALL SIX PASS on the new chip:

  I1  the band selection never changes while the amplifier is keyed
  I2  the amplifier is only ever keyed while the band is locked
  I3  during bypass-snoop every TX output stays inactive
  I4  the band outputs always agree with the firmware's own idea of the band
  I5  every band change happens with the amplifier cold
  I6  the T/R relay only closes after the band relay has settled

So the things that protect the hardware are behaving correctly on the new chip.
This blocker is about the amplifier being over-cautious, which is the safe
direction to be wrong in.
